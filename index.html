The company is pumping more oil every year

Output is measured in barrels of oil equivalent per day — gas converted into the amount of oil holding the same energy, so everything can be added up. The black line shows how fast production grows each year.

Revenue follows the barrels

Revenue depends on how much is sold and the price received. Because most costs are fixed per well, extra barrels arrive at a high margin — which is why the margin line stays flat as the company grows.

Each barrel is cheap to lift and sells for a lot more

The all-in cost is about $17.77 per barrel against a realised price near $62.90. The gap between the bars and the line is the profit on every barrel.

Where the money goes

Starting from every dollar of sales, this peels back one layer of cost at a time: production costs, then operating costs, then the charge for wells wearing out, then interest and tax.

From spending phase to harvest phase

Free cash flow is what is left after running the business and drilling new wells — the money genuinely available to repay debt. It turns positive in 2026 and grows from there.

The debt taken on to grow gets paid back

Net debt ÷ EBITDA answers one question: how many years of earnings would it take to repay everything owed? Under 2x is generally comfortable for a producer.

From future cash flows to today's equity value

A discounted cash flow adds up the cash the business should produce and converts it into today's money, because a dollar received in 2030 is worth less than a dollar today.

What the share is worth under each method

Two independent methods, then the average of the two. The dashed line is where the share actually trades.
